# General Principles

This domain holds principles and practices that genuinely apply across several life areas. Ideas that fit naturally in one domain should remain there instead of being duplicated here. These principles are working commitments, not doctrines: later sources may refine, limit, or challenge them.

## Build well-being around the quality of choices, not guaranteed outcomes

Outcomes, possessions, status, health, and reputation matter, but none is fully secure. A more resilient foundation is the quality of judgment, intention, and action brought to changing circumstances.

This principle does not make outcomes irrelevant. It changes where identity and self-evaluation are anchored.

Practical implications:

- define success partly by how the situation was handled, not only by whether the preferred result occurred
- pursue preferred outcomes seriously without treating them as proof of personal worth
- after a setback, review the decisions and conduct that were actually available to choose

## Put effort where agency exists and release entitlement to the result

Distinguish what can still be influenced from what has already happened or depends on other people, chance, and systems. Then act fully on the available part without assuming that effort creates a right to a particular outcome.

Practical implications:

- name the next available action instead of repeatedly rehearsing the uncontrollable part
- make plans with uncertainty rather than waiting for certainty
- stop spending effort on mentally renegotiating facts that cannot now be changed

## Treat first impressions as hypotheses, not verdicts

An immediate interpretation is information, not necessarily truth. Creating a pause between impression and judgment makes clearer perception and better action possible.

Practical implications:

- separate the observable event from the story attached to it
- ask what evidence would support or weaken the first interpretation
- allow emotions to be present without requiring them to settle the meaning of the event

## Develop character as a set of trainable capacities

Character becomes more actionable when expressed through capacities such as:

- wisdom: seeing the situation clearly and choosing what is worth pursuing
- courage: doing what matters despite fear, discomfort, or social pressure
- justice: treating people fairly and accounting for shared interests
- moderation: keeping desire and behavior proportionate rather than ruled by excess

These capacities develop through repeated choices in ordinary situations, not only through reflection about ideals.

## Hold acceptance and action together

Acceptance is accurate contact with reality; it is not approval, resignation, or passivity. Action is responsibility for what can be done now; it is not a guarantee of control.

Use both questions:

1. What reality needs to be acknowledged without further bargaining?
2. What worthwhile action remains available?

## Include other people in the definition of flourishing

A good life cannot be evaluated only by inner calm, achievement, or self-development. Fairness, care, reliability, and contribution to shared life are part of flourishing itself.

Practical implications:

- include effects on other people when evaluating a decision
- treat justice and care as everyday practices, not only responses to exceptional situations
- do not use personal tranquility as a reason to withdraw from responsibilities that properly belong to you
